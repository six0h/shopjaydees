The copy was doing exactly what we told it to do, and that was the problem. We told Ellie "no pressure", gave her a soft ask ("worth a quick chat?"), and left her nothing concrete to say beyond "custom apparel". The result was polite, safe, and easy to ignore. Nobody says no to an email like that. Nobody says yes either.

What we changed, inside your guardrails

Every rule from July stands: no product names, no prices, no catalogue, correct season only, and every draft still machine-checked before it reaches your queue.

  • Confident voice. Ellie now writes like someone with a genuine reason to reach out this week, not someone apologizing for emailing.
  • Real urgency only. Every email anchors to the actual fall calendar: orders that start now arrive before the season, orders that start later may not. Invented discounts, made-up deadlines, and "spots are filling up" are banned outright and machine-checked.
  • A real question at the end. Every email closes with one specific question that can be answered in one line, for example "Want me to put a no-obligation fall quote together for you this week?" The soft closers are gone.
  • The third email is now a break-up email. Instead of a third "just checking in", Ellie says she is wrapping up her fall list and asks whether to close the file. It is more honest, and in cold outreach it is reliably the email that gets answered.
  • Two versions are now being tested. Half the leads get an email that opens with the fall timing; half get one that opens with the personal observation and asks straight away. Each lead is tagged, so in a few weeks we will know which style earns more replies instead of guessing.
Nothing changes in your day. Drafts still land in your Ready for Review queue, and nothing sends without your approval.

Two asksYour call on both

Ask one: a small loosening of the product rule

Right now Ellie may only ever say "custom apparel". That was the right call when she was inventing products, and the machine checks that stopped that are staying either way. But it also means a plumbing company and a soccer club get the same abstract words, and abstract copy is easy to ignore.

Proposal: allow segment-level words only, so Ellie can say "crew gear" to a contractor, "team wear" or "warm-up gear" to a club, and "staff apparel" to an office or restaurant. Still banned, exactly as today: specific garments, styles, fabrics, colours, prices, and anything Jaydees doesn't have ready to send.

Illustration onlyHow that would read (does not exist until you approve it)

"With your crew back at full tilt this fall, matching crew gear is the fastest way to look like the bigger outfit on site. Want me to put a fall quote together this week?"

If you'd rather keep "custom apparel" only, that is completely fine. The new copy on page two already ships without it.

Ask two: turn on open tracking

Open tracking is currently off, so when a lead goes quiet we can't tell whether they never opened the email (a delivery question) or opened it and passed (a copy question). We recommend turning it on for the new campaigns so the two-version test reads cleanly.

Small trade-off to be upfront about: tracking uses an invisible pixel, which very occasionally nudges spam filters. At our sending volumes we think the data is worth it, and we can switch it off again at any time.

What happens next

  1. New drafts with the sharper copy are already flowing into your normal Ready for Review queue. Approve, edit, or reject exactly as before.
  2. In two to three weeks we compare reply rates between the two versions and keep the winner.
  3. If you approve the segment-level wording, we ship it the same day behind the same machine checks.
